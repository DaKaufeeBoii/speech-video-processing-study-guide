3. Define image segmentation and classify its major categories based on region-based and boundary-based approaches.
Image segmentation is the process of dividing a digital image into multiple meaningful regions or segments based on similar properties such as colour, intensity, texture, and boundaries.
The main objective is to simplify image representation and identify important objects or regions for further image analysis.

Major Categories of Image Segmentation
Image segmentation is broadly classified into two major approaches:
A. Region-Based Segmentation
Region-based segmentation groups neighbouring pixels that have similar characteristics, such as intensity, colour, or texture.
Techniques:
Region Growing: Starts with a seed pixel and adds neighbouring pixels with similar intensity values.
Region Splitting: Divides an image into smaller regions when the pixels do not satisfy a homogeneity condition.
Region Merging: Combines adjacent regions having similar propertie
\
Example: Separating a green leaf from the background based on similar colour and intensity values.
B. Boundary-Based Segmentation
Boundary-based segmentation identifies object boundaries by detecting sudden changes in image intensity or colour.
Techniques:
Edge Detection: Identifies boundaries using intensity changes.
Sobel Operator: Detects horizontal and vertical edges using gradient masks.
Canny Edge Detection: Detects strong and weak edges while reducing noise.
Laplacian Operator: Detects edges using second-order intensity derivatives.

Example: Identifying the boundary of a vehicle in a traffic surveillance image.
Difference Between Region-Based and Boundary-Based Segmentation

Image segmentation divides an image into meaningful regions for effective image analysis. Region-based approaches group pixels with similar properties, whereas boundary-based approaches identify object boundaries using intensity changes. Both approaches are important in computer vision and digital image processing.
4. Explain the principles of change detection in video sequences and interpret its role in dynamic scene analysis.
Change detection is a computer vision technique used to identify differences between consecutive video frames or between a reference frame and the current frame.
It detects changes in object positions, movement, appearance, and environmental conditions.
Main objective: To identify moving objects and analyse dynamic changes in a video scene.
 Principles of Change Detection
The major principles involved in change detection are:
A. Frame Differencing
Frame differencing identifies changes by subtracting two consecutive video frames.
Mathematical equation:
Dx,y=Itx,y-It-1x,y


B. Background Subtraction
Background subtraction compares the current frame with a background model to identify moving objects.
Mathematical equation:
Dx,y=Itx,y-Bx,y

C. Thresholding
Thresholding converts the difference image into a binary image.
Mx,y=1,Dx,y>T0,Dx,y≤T
Where (T) is the threshold value.
1 = Motion or change detected.
0 = No significant change detected.
D. Optical Flow
Optical flow estimates the apparent movement of pixels between consecutive frames.
It provides information about the direction and speed of object movement.

Role in Dynamic Scene Analysis
Change detection plays an important role in analysing dynamic environments.
Moving Object Detection: Identifies moving vehicles, people, and animals.
Traffic Monitoring: Detects vehicle movement and traffic congestion.
Video Surveillance: Identifies suspicious activities and unauthorized movement.
Activity Recognition: Analyses human actions and behaviour.
Environmental Monitoring: Detects changes such as floods, fires, and other environmental events.
Practical Example
Consider a CCTV camera monitoring a parking area.
Frame 1: The parking area is empty.
Frame 2: A car enters the parking area.
Frame 3: Frame differencing identifies changes in pixel values.
Thresholding separates the moving car from unchanged regions.
The system detects and tracks the vehicle.
Change detection identifies variations between video frames using frame differencing, background subtraction, thresholding, and optical flow. It helps detect moving objects, analyse dynamic scenes, and understand object behaviour. It is widely used in video surveillance, traffic monitoring, robotics, and intelligent transportation systems.

6. Analyze image and video matting techniques and evaluate their effectiveness in foreground extraction under complex backgrounds.
Image and video matting is a computer vision technique used to separate foreground objects from their backgrounds by estimating the transparency (alpha value) of each pixel.
Unlike simple image segmentation, which classifies pixels as foreground or background, matting estimates the degree of transparency of pixels, especially around object boundaries such as hair, fur, glass, and transparent objects.
Main objective: To extract foreground objects accurately while preserving fine details and transparency under complex background conditions.
Mathematical Model of Image Matting
The basic image matting equation is:
Ix,y=αx,yFx,y+1-αx,yBx,y
Where:
(I(x,y)) = Observed image pixel.
(F(x,y)) = Foreground colour.
(B(x,y)) = Background colour.
\(\alpha(x,y)\) = Transparency value.
The alpha value ranges between 0 and 1:


Image Matting Techniques
Image matting techniques estimate the foreground and background contributions at each pixel.
A. Chroma Key Matting
Uses a green or blue background.
Identifies pixels having a predefined background colour.
Removes the background and retains the foreground object.
Advantages:
Simple and computationally efficient.
Suitable for studio environments.
Widely used in film production.
Limitations:
Requires a uniform background.
Difficult when foreground objects contain similar colours.
May produce unwanted edges around hair and transparent objects.

B. Bayesian Matting
Bayesian matting estimates foreground, background, and alpha values using probability distributions.
It considers the colour distributions of foreground and background pixels.
Working steps:
Identify known foreground and background regions.
Estimate colour distributions.
Calculate the probability of foreground and background colours.
Estimate the alpha value.
Generate the final foreground matte.
Advantages: Handles complex colour variations and produces accurate transparency estimates.
Limitations: Computationally expensive and dependent on accurate initial foreground/background information.
C. Closed-Form Matting
Closed-form matting estimates alpha values by minimizing a mathematical cost function based on local colour consistency.
Advantages:
Preserves fine image details.
Produces smooth foreground boundaries.
Effective for hair and fine structures.
Limitations:
Can be computationally expensive for large images.
May struggle with highly complex textures and colour similarities.
D. Deep Learning-Based Matting
Deep learning techniques use convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and encoder-decoder architectures to predict alpha mattes.
Working steps:
Input the original image.
Extract important visual features using CNN layers.
Learn foreground and background characteristics.
Predict pixel-wise alpha values.
Generate the foreground object with transparency information.
Advantages:
Handles complex backgrounds.
Preserves fine details.
Provides high-quality foreground extraction.
Limitations:
Requires large training datasets.
High computational requirements.
Performance may decrease on unfamiliar backgrounds.
Video Matting Techniques
Video matting extends image matting to consecutive video frames. It extracts foreground objects while maintaining temporal consistency.
A. Background Subtraction-Based Matting
This technique compares each video frame with a reference background.
Working steps:
Capture the background frame.
Compare the current frame with the background.
Identify moving foreground objects.
Calculate foreground transparency.
Extract the moving object.
Advantages: Simple, fast, and suitable for static-camera surveillance.
Limitations: Sensitive to lighting changes, shadows, and background movement.

B. Optical Flow-Based Video Matting
Optical flow estimates pixel movement between consecutive frames.
Working steps:
Identify foreground objects in the initial frame.
Calculate optical flow between consecutive frames.
Track pixel movements.
Propagate alpha values to subsequent frames.
Refine the matte to maintain smooth boundaries.
Advantages:
Maintains object movement information.
Reduces frame-to-frame inconsistency.
Useful for tracking moving objects.
Limitations:
Sensitive to fast motion and occlusion.
Incorrect optical flow may produce inaccurate boundaries.
C. Deep Learning-Based Video Matting
Deep learning models use CNNs, recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and temporal attention mechanisms to estimate alpha mattes across video frames.
Working steps:
Input consecutive video frames.
Extract spatial features using CNNs.
Learn temporal relationships between frames.
Estimate alpha mattes.
Refine foreground boundaries and maintain temporal consistency.
Advantages:
Handles complex backgrounds.
Preserves hair and fine object details.
Produces smoother foreground extraction.
Limitations:
Requires high computational resources.
Needs large video training datasets.
May introduce temporal flickering under difficult conditions.
