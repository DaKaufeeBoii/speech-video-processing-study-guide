
1. Discuss the concept of multi-dimensional sampling theory.
Multi-dimensional sampling theory is an extension of one-dimensional signal sampling to signals defined over two or more dimensions, such as images (2D), videos (3D: x, y, time), and volumetric data (medical imaging). It explains how continuous multi-dimensional signals can be converted into discrete samples and later reconstructed without loss of information.
In one-dimensional sampling, a continuous signal is sampled along time. In multi-dimensional sampling, signals are sampled along multiple axes.
 For example:
Image → sampled along x and y axes
Video → sampled along x, y, and time axes
Thus, a continuous signal f(x, y) becomes a discrete signal f(mT₁, nT₂), where T₁ and T₂ are sampling intervals.
Multi-Dimensional Nyquist CriterionFor perfect reconstruction, the signal must be bandlimited in all dimensions. The sampling frequency in each dimension must satisfy:Sampling rate ≥ 2 × maximum frequency in that dimension
For a 2D signal (image):
Sampling must satisfy Nyquist along both horizontal and vertical directions
If fx and fy are maximum frequencies, then: fsx ≥ 2fx and fsy ≥ 2fy
Failure to meet this condition results in aliasing (overlapping of spectral components).
Sampling Process
In multi-dimensional sampling, a continuous signal fxyis multiplied by a sampling function, which is an impulse train (also called a Dirac comb) in multiple dimensions. This sampling function places impulses at regular intervals along each axis.
Mathematically:
Sampling function: δsxy=mnδx-mT1y-nT2
This function is called a 2D Dirac comb (impulse train).It creates an infinite grid of impulse points in the x-y plane.
Each impulse is located at:
xy=mT1nT2
Where:
m,n→ integers (…, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, …) 
T1→ sampling interval in x-direction 
T2→ sampling interval in y-direction 
δ(x, y)
δxyis a 2D impulse (Dirac delta function) 
It is zero everywhere except at the origin 
It “picks” the value of a signal at a specific point
Sampled signal:
fsxy=fxy⋅δsxy
Explanation:
The continuous signal exists over all values of xand y. 
The impulse train “picks” values only at discrete locations mT1nT2. 
The result is a set of discrete samples arranged in a grid (lattice structure). 
This grid of sampled points forms the basis of digital images, where each sample corresponds to a pixel value.
Sampling Lattices (Grid Structures)Sampling in multiple dimensions is represented using lattices:
Rectangular Grid: Most common (pixels in images)
Hexagonal Grid: More efficient (better circular symmetry, less redundancy)
The arrangement affects sampling efficiency and reconstruction accuracy.
 Reconstruction of Multi-Dimensional SignalsThe original signal is reconstructed using interpolation methods such as:
Ideal sinc interpolation (theoretical)
Practical filters (bilinear, bicubic interpolation in images)
Reconstruction is performed independently or jointly across dimensions.
Aliasing in Multi-DimensionsIf sampling rates are insufficient:
Spectral overlap occurs in frequency domain
Causes distortion such as moiré patterns in images
Thus, anti-aliasing filters are applied before sampling.
Example: Image Sampling Grid
Consider a grayscale image:
Continuous image f(x, y) is sampled into pixels
Each pixel represents intensity at discrete coordinates
Example: A 512 × 512 image means 512 samples along x and y
If sampling is too low → image appears blurred or distortedIf sampling is adequate → image is clear and reconstructable
Applications
Image processing and computer vision
Video compression and streaming
Medical imaging (CT, MRI)
Remote sensing and satellite imaging






2. Illustrate with a block diagram and justify the transformation process.
The transformation process converts a signal from one domain (usually time/spatial) into another domain (frequency/subband) to simplify analysis and processing.
Steps in Transformation Process
Input Signal (Continuous Domain) 
A real-world signal such as speech, image, or video is initially continuous in time or space. 
Sampling 
The continuous signal is converted into a discrete signal using a sampling process. 
Sampling rate must satisfy the Nyquist criterion to avoid aliasing. 
Quantization (if digital system) 
The sampled values are quantized into finite levels for digital representation. 
Transformation Block (Core Stage) 
Mathematical transforms like DFT, FFT, DCT, or Wavelet Transform are applied. 
This converts the signal into frequency or subband domain. 
Processing in Transform Domain 
Operations such as filtering, compression, feature extraction, or enhancement are performed efficiently. 
Inverse Transformation 
The processed signal is converted back to the original domain using inverse transforms (e.g., IDFT). 
Output Signal 
The final reconstructed signal is obtained, often with improved quality or extracted information. 
Transformation Process
Simplifies Analysis: Many signals are complex in time domain but become simple in frequency domain (e.g., sinusoids become peaks). 
Efficient Processing: Filtering and convolution are easier (multiplication in frequency domain). 
Feature Extraction: Important features (e.g., formants in speech, edges in images) are more distinguishable. 
Compression: Transforms like DCT concentrate energy, enabling efficient coding (used in JPEG/MPEG). 
Noise Reduction: Noise can be isolated and removed more effectively in transform domain.


Step-by-Step  Continuous-Time Input
CT Input (Continuous-Time Signal) 
The input is an analog signal (e.g., speech, audio, sensor signal) that varies continuously with time. 
Sampling 
Converts the continuous signal into a discrete-time (DT) signal. 
Done at a sampling frequency fs. 
Must satisfy Nyquist criterion:
fs≥2fmax
to avoid aliasing.
Quantization 
Converts discrete-time samples into digital values (finite levels). 
Introduces quantization error/noise due to rounding. 
Digital Signal (Binary Representation) 
After quantization, the signal becomes suitable for digital systems (stored as bits). 
Digital Processor (DSP Block) 
Core processing stage where algorithms are applied: 
Filtering 
Compression 
Feature extraction 
Enhancement 
Examples: FFT, filtering, coding, ML-based processing. 
Dequantization 
Converts digital values back into approximate discrete amplitude levels. 
Reconstruction Filter (DAC Stage) 
Converts discrete signal back to continuous-time output. 
A low-pass filter removes high-frequency components introduced during sampling. 
CT Output  (Continuous-Time Output)
Final analog signal, ideally a close approximation of the original input.


3.Evaluate the influence of human visual system properties such as luminance sensitivity, contrast perception, and color interpretation on image representation and compression techniques.
Human visual system (HVS) characteristics strongly guide how images are represented, sampled, and compressed, allowing systems to reduce data while preserving perceived quality.




The HVS is more sensitive to luminance (Y) than chrominance (Cb, Cr). 
Humans detect fine details primarily through brightness variations, not color. 
Impact on Representation:
Images are converted from RGB → YCbCr color space. 
Luminance (Y) is preserved with high precision. 
Impact on Compression:
More bits are allocated to luminance, fewer to chrominance. 
Example: Chroma subsampling (4:2:0, 4:2:2) reduces color resolution without noticeable loss. 
Used in standards like JPEG, MPEG, H.264.
2. Contrast Sensitivity (Perception of Differences)


 

The HVS is sensitive to contrast changes, especially at mid spatial frequencies. 
Less sensitive to: 
Very high frequencies (fine noise) 
Very low frequencies (smooth regions) 
Impact on Representation:
Images are analyzed in the frequency domain (DCT, FFT). 
Important visual features (edges, textures) are preserved. 
Impact on Compression:
High-frequency components can be quantized heavily or discarded. 
Low-contrast details can be compressed aggressively. 
Example: 
In JPEG, DCT coefficients are quantized based on perceptual importance. 
Leads to efficient compression with minimal perceived loss.




3. Color Perception (Chromatic Sensitivity)
 


Human eyes are less sensitive to color variations than brightness. 
Color perception depends on cones, which are fewer than rods. 
Impact on Representation:
Use of perceptual color models (YCbCr, Lab). 
Separation of intensity and color information. 
Impact on Compression:
Aggressive compression of chrominance channels. 
Minor color distortions are less noticeable. 
Enables higher compression ratios.
4. Overall Influence on Image Compression Techniques
Key Design Principles:
Perceptual Redundancy Removal: Remove information not noticeable to the human eye. 
Transform Coding: 
DCT (JPEG), Wavelets (JPEG2000) 
Quantization Based on HVS: 
Perceptual weighting matrices used. 
Bit Allocation Strategy: 
More bits → important visual components (edges, luminance) 
Fewer bits → less noticeable components (color, noise)

4. Design and justify a digital video representation system, incorporating sampling theory, frame structure, and temporal redundancy reduction techniques.
A digital video representation system converts real-world continuous video signals into a compact, efficient, and reconstructable digital form. The design involves three major components: sampling theory, frame structure, and temporal redundancy reduction.

Digital Video Representation System how a real-world video (continuous signal) is converted, compressed, transmitted, and reconstructed for display.
1. Video Acquisition (Camera)
Captures real-world motion as a continuous video signal 
Represented as: f(x, y, t) 
x, y → spatial coordinates (image) 
t → time (video) 
 Output: raw analog-like video signal
2. Sampling & Quantization
This is the digitization stage.
Spatial Sampling
Samples image in (x, y) grid (pixels) 
Converts continuous image → discrete pixels 
Temporal Sampling
Captures frames at intervals of time (fps) 
Example: 30 frames per second 
Quantization
Converts pixel intensity into digital values (0–255, etc.) 
Output: digital video frames
3. Encoding (Compression)
Goal: reduce data size
Removes:
Spatial redundancy (within a frame) 
Temporal redundancy (between frames) 
Techniques:
Motion estimation & compensation 
Prediction coding 
Entropy coding (Huffman, arithmetic, etc.) 
 Output: compressed bitstream

4. Transmission / Storage
Sends or stores compressed video 
Mediums: 
Internet streaming 
Cloud storage 
Disk storage 
Formats: MP4, H.264, etc. 
5. Decoding & Reconstruction
Reverse process at receiver side:
Decode compressed data 
Reconstruct frames 
Apply inverse quantization 
Display video on screen 
 Output: viewable video
Supporting Concepts in Bottom Panels
A. Sampling Theory (3D Signal)
Video is treated as a 3D signal: f(x, y, t)
Spatial sampling:fs≥2fmax(Nyquist rule) 
Temporal sampling: 
Frame rate depends on motion 
Example: 1920×1080 at 30 fps 
B. Frame Structure
Three main frame types:
I-frame: Full image (independent) 
P-frame: Predicts from previous frame 
B-frame: Uses past + future frames 
Example: I → B → B → P → B → P
C. Temporal Redundancy Reduction
Reduces repeated information across frames:
Frame differencing (only changes stored) 
Motion estimation (find movement vectors) 
Motion compensation (predict next frame) 
Prediction coding (encode only error) 
This is the core of video compression (H.264/H.265)


2. Sampling Theory in Video Representation
Video is a 3D signal:
2D spatial dimensions: x,y
1D temporal dimension: t
(a) Spatial Sampling
Converts continuous image into pixels. 
Sampling rate must satisfy Nyquist criterion:
fs≥2fmax
Typical resolutions: 
SD: 720×480 
HD: 1920×1080 
4K: 3840×2160 
 Prevents spatial aliasing (blurring, jagged edges)

(b) Temporal Sampling (Frame Rate)
Video is sampled into frames per second (fps) 
Typical frame rates:
24 fps → cinematic video 
30 fps → standard broadcast 
60 fps → smooth motion 
Prevents temporal aliasing (jerky motion)
(c) Color Sampling
RGB → YCbCr conversion 
Y = Luminance (brightness) 
Cb, Cr = Chrominance (color difference) 
Chroma subsampling (4:2:2, 4:2:0)✔ Reduces data using human visual system limitation (less sensitivity to color than brightness) 
3. Frame Structure in Digital Video
Video is organized into frames:
Types of Frames
(a) I-Frame (Intra-coded frame)
Self-contained image 
No dependency on other frames 
Acts as reference frame 
(b) P-Frame (Predictive frame)
Encoded using previous I or P frame 
Stores motion differences 
(c) B-Frame (Bidirectional frame)
Uses both past and future frames for prediction 
Highest compression efficiency 
GOP (Group of Pictures)
Example structure:
I  B  B  P  B  B  P  B  B  I
✔ Reduces redundancy across time
4. Temporal Redundancy Reduction Techniques
Video contains high temporal redundancy (similar consecutive frames).
(a) Frame Differencing
Encode only difference between frames:
Dt=Ft-Ft-1
✔ Simple but less efficient
(b) Motion Estimation
Detect motion of objects between frames 
Divides frame into blocks (e.g., 16×16) 
Output: Motion vectors
Example:
Object shifts right by 5 pixels → motion vector (5,0) 
(c) Motion Compensation
Reconstruct current frame using: 
Previous frame + motion vectors 
Ft≈Ft-1+MotionVector
Major compression gain
(d) Transform Coding (Optional enhancement)
DCT (Discrete Cosine Transform) 
Converts spatial data into frequency domain 
Removes less important high-frequency components 
5. Justification of Design Choices
✔ Sampling Theory
Ensures faithful reconstruction of video 
Prevents aliasing in space and time 
✔ Frame Structure
Separates independent and dependent frames 
Enables random access (I-frames) 
✔ Temporal Redundancy Reduction
Exploits similarity between frames 
Reduces bitrate significantly (up to 90% compression) 

6. Outcome
A modern digital video system achieves:
High visual quality 
Reduced storage size 
Efficient streaming 
Real-time transmission capability 
7. Key Standards (Examples)
MPEG-2 (broadcast TV) 
H.264/AVC (YouTube, streaming) 
H.265/HEVC (4K/8K compression)
